Design · Waves panel

Stack any group of signals.

Select a group and press Shift A. The group's row becomes one area: every signal in it is a layer, and the top edge is their sum at each moment. Eight one-bit issue valids become instructions issued per cycle, split by pipe. Zoomed out, each layer shows its signal's time-weighted mean, so the layers still add up to the mean of the total. Everything comes from the trace: a stack needs no declarations, and Volna refuses no group.

Design, revised 29 September 2026 · implemented: all three stages landed by 1 October 2026 · builds on signal groups, analog rows, row colours and undo · demo on per-cycle values of eight C910 issue valids from a CoreMark recording

How it works

A stack is a way to draw a group.

Groups already fold, move, cut, paste, rename and ungroup. Stacking changes only how the group's own row is drawn. The members stay ordinary rows below it, drawn as before, with a swatch of their layer's colour before the name.

  1. Any group, one key.Shift A or Stacked area in the group's Draw menu. The group row grows to 3×, the height an analog row opens at, and goes back to 1× when unstacked unless it was resized in between. A still turns the selected signals into analog plots.
  2. Every signal counts as its number.A one-bit signal counts 0 or 1. A vector or real counts the number its row's format reads, the same number its analog plot draws: hex and unsigned read unsigned, signed decimal reads signed, float and real read the value. Signals in nested groups count too. Rows with no number, such as text, strings, events, transaction lanes and clocks, are left out; their value cell says not stacked, and the header counts only the layers drawn.
  3. List order is stack order.The layers read top to bottom as the rows do, so the last member sits on the baseline. To reorder, drag a row. Only the baseline layer and the total can be read against the axis, so put the part that matters most at the bottom. Volna never reorders layers by size, because a layer that changes place as it grows cannot be followed.
  4. Neighbours always differ.Layer colours come from the pipeline stage ladder: hue walks in list order and lightness alternates, so two adjacent layers never look alike, whatever the order. A member with its own row colour keeps it. A one-pixel gap of canvas separates layers; they have no borders.
  5. Zero stays at zero.Positive parts stack up from zero and negative parts stack down from it, as in a diverging stack. When both signs occur, a line marks the net sum. The scale runs from the whole trace's lowest sum to its highest, rounded to clean numbers, so the plot does not move while you pan.
  6. Zoomed out, means add up.Where a pixel column spans several changes, each layer shows its member's time-weighted mean over the column. Means over one span add up, so the stack's top is the column's mean total. A faint band above it reaches the total's own peak in that column. The next section explains why per-layer extremes cannot be stacked.
  7. Every value, one hover away.The group's value cell shows the sum at the cursor, Σ 5, and each member's cell shows its own value. Hovering the plot lists every layer top to bottom with its value and share, then the total; the layer under the pointer is highlighted. Where a column holds changes, the list says its values are means over the column's time span. Shift ← → step through the total's changes.
  8. Unknown stays unknown.Members change at different times; the stack is built on the union of their change times, never by sample index. Where a member is X or Z, or has no value yet, the layers below it are drawn and the rest of the column takes the undefined colour. The sum reads X. A missing value is never read as zero.
  9. Stacking is an edit; looking is not.Stacking and unstacking are undoable steps (Stack issue), and so is the peak band's switch. Moving a layer is the row move that already exists. Hover, cursor, zoom and folding are navigation and never make a step.

Zoomed out

Means add up. Extremes do not.

Volna's analog painter keeps a line exact at any zoom by drawing each pixel column's first, last, lowest and highest value (M4, Jugel et al.). That does not carry over to a stack: the maximum of a sum is not the sum of the maxima. The two pictures stack the eight pipes of the list stretch, 1,024 cycles in 64 columns of 16 cycles. The white line in both is the true peak of the total in each column, the most instructions issued in any one of its cycles.

Means are exact. Over one column's time span, the time-weighted mean of the total equals the sum of the members' means. The stack's area is the number of events, its top is the column's average total, and each layer is its member's average: for a one-bit valid, the share of cycles it was high. A member's mean is its integral at the column's right edge minus the integral at its left edge, over the column's duration.

Means hide bursts, so the peak is drawn apart. The band above the stack reaches the total's own maximum in the column, never a sum of per-layer maxima. When a column holds one step or less, the peak equals the top of the stack and the band vanishes.

Two paths, as for analog rows. When the members have fewer visible changes than a quarter of the plot width, every step is drawn exactly at its time, from a merge of their changes. Otherwise the painter draws one column per pixel. Walking the visible changes costs time in proportion to them, so long layers read block integrals instead (stage 2), and a zoomed-out frame costs about members × width × log(changes).

Plan

Three commits, each useful on its own.

Every commit builds, passes cargo test -p volna-core and volna/volna/check.sh, and updates the tests and documentation it touches. No commit adds a feature flag or code that only a later commit calls. Where a stage changes the screen, its picture shows that stage and the ones before, painted by this page from the demo's data, and red rings number the new elements as its list does. VTR, its C API and the client–server protocol do not change: a stack is presentation over histories that local and remote sessions already deliver.

Every stage has landed. The four stackable scopes of volna/volna/examples/feature_showcase.vtr show the whole feature.

Why this order. Stage 1 is the whole feature on traces of ordinary size. Stage 2 is the performance gate for long histories, set by measurement. Stage 3 adds the peak band, which needs a summary of the total that only makes sense once stage 2's summaries exist.

Stage 1 Landed

Stacked groups

A selected group stacks with Shift A or from its menu. Everything in the rules except the peak band is in this stage: layers in list order with ladder colours, diverging parts, exact steps when zoomed in and column means when zoomed out, the sum in the value cell, the hover readout and undo. The four stackable scopes of volna/volna/examples/feature_showcase.vtr exercise it: pipeline stage occupancy (bits), a power breakdown sampled every nanosecond (reals, beside a text row that stays out), DMA queue depths (counts, one unknown during the fault) and battery currents of both signs.

  2. The header line under the group's name: stacked · 8 layers.
  3. The sum at the cursor in the group's value cell.
  4. A swatch per member, in its layer's colour.

Stage 2 Landed

Busy views stay fast

Stage 1 walks every visible change: stack_cost measured 41 ms per zoomed-out frame at 10⁶ changes per member and 409 ms at 10⁷. This stage gives long layers a summary of their integrals, and a zoomed-out frame now takes 3.5 ms at 10⁷. Nothing changes on screen.

Committed

Core
IntegralSummary in wave/stack.rs: per block of 64 changes, the member's integral since its first change (an f64, summed with Kahan's compensation) and how many of those spans are undefined for a while (a u32), 12 bytes per block or about 0.19 bytes per change; the plan's 0.125 left out the undefined count. A column's mean is the difference of two prefixes, each a block entry and at most half a block read from the history; a prefix just after the previous one continues it, and each column's search starts where the last column's density points. A layer reads its summary when it has more than 16 visible changes per column, and walks otherwise. Built by LoadRequest::Integral on the load worker for every layer of at least 16,384 changes in a stack of more than 16,384 changes in all, held by the Document by history and reading with a weak handle to the history, shared by the panels that stack it, charged to the memory budget and released when no visible stack wants it or its history goes. One-bit members get it too, since they have no analog summary. While one builds, a frame that would walk more than 16,384 changes shows Summarizing… rather than walk on the UI thread.
Undo
None: summaries are derived data.
Bench
stack_cost with the summaries: a zoomed-out frame of eight members takes 0.84, 1.4, 1.8 and 3.5 ms at 10⁴, 10⁵, 10⁶ and 10⁷ changes per member (10⁴ is walked, below the summaries' threshold); 1/100 of the trace 0.16 to 1.4 ms. The eight summaries build in 4.3, 43 and 436 ms on the worker from 10⁵ to 10⁷. Recorded in docs/RATIONALE.md next to stage 1's numbers.
Tests
tests/stack.rs: prefixes equal a direct walk at and around every block boundary and at the last change; column means from summaries equal the walked ones on random histories within 10⁻⁹, with the same undefined columns and change counts; a long layer's frame waits while its summary builds; a summary of another reading is not used; a long stack's summaries build on the worker, go when it is unstacked, and its histories are released with its rows.

Stage 3 Landed

Peak of the total

Means hide bursts. A faint band above a zoomed-out stack reaches the busiest moment of each column, so a stack never looks calmer than the design was. In the feature showcase, the core's one-nanosecond power glitches rise above the power stack's means as this band.

  1. The peak band above the stack, to the total's maximum in each column; below the negative parts, the total's minimum.
  2. The peak in the readout, after the means of a column that holds changes: means over 2.09 ns · peak 58.72, and · low when the total goes below zero.
  3. Peak of total, a check in the Draw section of the group's menu, on by default.

Committed

Core
GroupStyle::Stack { peak: bool } and WaveModel::set_peak, reached through MenuAction::Peak. stack::peaks finds each column's extremes of the total exactly, in one merged walk over the changes in view, for views of up to 16,384 changes. For busier views the TotalSummary walk, which already set the scale, also keeps the total's minimum and maximum per block of time (at most 65,536 blocks over the trace, with coarser levels of 16 above, 24 bytes each, about 1.7 MB), and TotalSummary::peaks reads the coarsest level whose blocks fit a column. A column there reads every block it touches, so its band may reach up to one block past the column on either side, never below its own peak. No member summary can give either, because the maximum of a sum is not a sum of maxima. The walk and the peaks share one merged cursor over the layers (Merge). The painter draws the band only where it rises above the stack's top or below its bottom, and not over undefined columns.

Limits

Volna sums what you group.

Whether a sum means anything is your call. A trace does not say that two signals share a unit, or that they never count the same event twice. Every in-flight load holds a ROB entry and a load-queue entry, so stacking the two counts each load twice. Volna cannot see that from values, so it does not refuse such a stack. The header says what is summed (stacked · 8 layers), never what it means.

The format decides the number. A 64-bit valid vector in hex reads as one huge unsigned number, and the stack draws it. Set the member's format, or leave it out of the group. Stacks of ratios, such as misses per kilo-instruction, are not additive after averaging; they wait for derived signals.

Upper layers are hard to read. Only the baseline layer and the total sit on a common scale; every other layer is a length between two curves. The value cells, the hover readout and a deliberate baseline mitigate this, and any member can be drawn as its own analog row.

Thin layers vanish. The FPU pipes issue a few dozen instructions in the whole run, below a pixel at any zoom. The readout still lists them, and moving them out of the group clears the plot.

A column mixes signs. A signed member whose mean over a column is negative is drawn below zero for that column, even if it was positive for part of it. Zooming in shows the exact steps.

Many members are slow until stage 2. Stage 1's cost follows the visible changes, and its own benchmark sets the threshold for stage 2's summary.

Research

What perception studies and other tools say.

Perception. Cleveland and McGill (1984) discuss this chart as the aggregate line chart: only the bottom part and the total are judged by position on a common scale, and the other parts as lengths between two curves, which they found inaccurate. Byron and Wattenberg (2008) note that a zero baseline makes the sum easy to read at some cost to the layers, and ask for enough local contrast to tell layers apart without heavy borders. Thudt et al. (GI 2016) found no accuracy advantage for the zero-baseline stack, but readers rated it the most readable. Wilke's Fundamentals of Data Visualization makes the same point about stacked densities. These findings shape rules 3, 4 and 7: a deliberate baseline, one-pixel gaps and a ladder that keeps neighbours apart, and an exact value for every layer.

Taken: the zero and diverging offsets of d3 and Vega-Lite; Chrome's reading of several counters as one stacked area. Rejected: stacking by sample index (ECharts), because a group's signals change at different times; interpolation and inferred zeros (Plotly), because both invent values the trace does not hold; ordering by size or onset, because layers must keep their places; streamgraph and wiggle baselines; refusing stacks by declared units, because Volna stacks from the trace alone. Later: the 100% stack for shares, a visible-window scale, a hover gesture that moves a layer to the baseline for a moment (Thudt et al. found straightening a layer helped), derived signals for ratios, and heat lanes for groups whose parts should not be summed.
