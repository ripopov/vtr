Design · Hierarchy browser

Every scope's activity, exact, on any trace.

The theme proposal gives every scope row a meter for the share of its signals that change in the visible time range. That needs a yes or no for every signal in the design on every pan and zoom. Traces range from eight signals to millions, from a few thousand time steps to billions, from picoseconds to cycles, and from busy throughout to idle for most of the run. A signal is quiet in a window only when the window fits inside one of its silences, so the index keeps each signal's long silences. How long counts as long is decided by each writer block from its own byte budget, never by a constant in time units. The index therefore costs a bounded share of the file, answers every window at least that threshold wide exactly, and any window it cannot decide touches at most two blocks. The index is a .index sidecar, built once for a VTR or FST trace in bounded memory, so neither the simulator nor the trace pays for it.

Checked on 11 traces · 8 to 200,001 signals · 0.37 million to 1.95 billion changes · 1.2 MiB to 3.9 GiB · no wrong answer in 3,300 windows · undecided signals read in at most 36 ms

The problem

The meter asks about every signal, on every frame, of any trace.

The wave panel only loads the signals on its canvas. A scope meter covers every signal below the scope, so the top of the tree covers the whole design. The question is the same for each signal: does it change between t0 and t1? The answer is needed again whenever the window moves.

Two costs make the obvious answers fail on large traces. Reading the trace for a window costs in proportion to the data in it, so a zoomed-out window reads everything. Building any summary when the file is opened costs a pass over every change. On one thread, just collecting the change times takes 15 s for C910's 597 million changes and 44 s for the 1.95 billion of a 3.9 GiB analog trace. Both costs grow with the trace, and neither may be paid on every frame or at every open.

Nothing else about the trace may be assumed. The time unit can be 1 ps or one cycle. The run can be a microsecond or a second. Activity can be even, as in a core running a benchmark, or come in bursts separated by long sleeps. Any threshold written as a number of nanoseconds would be right for one trace and wrong for the next.

The idea

A window is quiet only inside a silence.

  1. Quiet means contained.A signal has no change in [t0, t1] exactly when its last change before t0 and its first change after t1 bound a silence that contains the window. The silences of one signal are disjoint, so at most one of them can hold a given window. That makes the question a lookup of one silence per signal, not a scan of changes.
  2. Only long silences matter.A window with w + 1 time steps fits only in a silence at least w + 2 long. Keep every silence longer than a threshold Δ and drop the rest. For any window with at least Δ time steps, the kept silences include every one that could contain it, so the answer is exact, not an estimate.
  3. What is left is a stretch list.Between the kept silences a signal is busy. Each busy stretch keeps three numbers: its first change, its last change and its largest inner gap. A clock is one stretch with a gap of half its period, a register written once is one stretch of length zero, and a signal that never changes after the first time step has no stretches at all.
  4. Four comparisons per signal.Binary search for the first stretch that ends at or after t0. If there is none, or it starts after t1, the window sits in a silence and the signal is quiet. If the window holds the stretch's first or last change, the signal is active. So it is when the window is at least as wide as the stretch's largest inner gap, or as the threshold where it lies. Otherwise the signal is undecided.
  5. The trace picks the scale.The writer cuts the run into blocks of a fixed number of records, so a block is short in time where the design is busy and long where it idles. Each block takes its own threshold Δ: the smallest power of two for which the silences ending in the block fit its budget, 1% of the block's compressed bytes on disk and 4% of them in memory. Δ never exceeds a quarter of the block's time span. Both rules count bytes and records, not time, so they read the same on a picosecond trace and on a cycle trace.
  6. Silences across blocks.A silence is kept when it is longer than the smallest threshold of the blocks it touches. A quiet window of at least that many time steps then always lies in a kept silence, so every window at least as wide as its blocks' smallest Δ is exact. A narrower window touches at most two blocks, because every block spans at least four of its thresholds.
  7. Undecided signals are read, locally.For those two blocks at most, the reader decodes only the undecided signals' columns. The writer bounds a block by its record count, so this read has the same ceiling on a 1 MiB trace and on a 4 GiB one. Across the eleven traces it took at most 36 ms on 16 threads. Decoding cost grows with window width, and the index is exact above Δ, so each method is cheap exactly where the other is expensive.
  8. Scopes count distinct signals.A variable often aliases a signal recorded elsewhere, so a scope counts signals, not variables. From each changing signal, walk up from the scopes of its variables and stop at the first scope already stamped with that signal. The walk is linear in the scope–signal pairs: 826,739 steps and 1 ms on C910, and 400,001 steps and 1.6 ms for 200,001 signals. Hui's colour-set-size method does the same in linear time for deeper trees.

The contract

Proof of the floor

No exact index can store less.

Slide a window of width w along one signal and record where the answer turns from active to quiet and back. Those turning points are exactly the ends of its silences longer than w + 1. Any structure that answers exactly for every window at least w wide must therefore reproduce the endpoints of every such silence. It can encode them in fewer bits, but it cannot keep fewer of them. The same holds for the per-scope counts, because the root's count changes at each of those endpoints. The stretch list stores those endpoints and nothing more, plus one gap per stretch.

The count of long silences is therefore the real price of exactness, and it is a property of the trace, not of the index. Silences longer than one time unit are nearly as many as the changes, so an exact index for one-step windows is the trace again. How fast the count falls as Δ grows differs from trace to trace, which is why each block measures it and picks its own Δ from a budget rather than from a fixed time. Exact answers for the narrowest windows must come from the trace, and an index can only make wide windows cheap. That is why the design uses both. Narrow windows are the only ones in which reading the trace is cheap.

The thresholds span eight orders of magnitude, from 2 time units to 134 million, and every one was chosen by the same rule. Within one trace they follow its blocks: C910's take 1,024 or 2,048 by their own silences, and on the bursty trace Δ is 0.1% of the longest block's span and 2.5% of the shortest. Traces that change on every sample keep one stretch per signal and are exact everywhere. * Two traces go over budget because their activity is too sparse or too wide for a quarter-block threshold: a signal of long_sparse changes about 225 times over 2.5 billion time units, and many_active has 200,001 signals in 1,000 cycles. There the cap binds by design. Staying within two blocks outranks the budget, and the overshoot is bounded by the signals with a silence longer than a quarter block.

The exact share depends on how widths are drawn, and a log-uniform draw weights windows narrower than a clock period as heavily as whole-run ones. The contract does not depend on it: a window is exact when its width reaches its blocks' Δ, and otherwise costs one read of at most two blocks. Decoding a trace's largest block completely on one thread took 38–434 ms, which bounds that read. Classification took 1.3–26 ns per signal on one thread for traces with over a thousand signals, 22 ns on C910. Extrapolated, a design of ten million signals would need about 15 ms on 16 threads, which Volna coalesces to one pass per frame off the UI thread.

Architecture

Where each piece lives.

  1. A sidecar for VTR and FST.The index lives next to the trace as <trace>.index: run.vtr.index, run.fst.index. No writer and no trace format changes, so recording pays nothing at simulation time and a trace is never rewritten after it is closed. The index is derived data: deleting it loses nothing, and rebuilding it gives the same bytes. Its format is the same for both sources. It reuses the VTR container (header, sections, directory, trailer) with its own magic and version, and holds one section per source block: the block's time range, bytes and Δ, the kept silences that end in it, and the largest gap of each stretch they close. Signals are the source's own identities: VTR signal ids, and FST handles, which already merge aliased variables. The sidecar format gets its own SPEC entry, a round-trip test and a RATIONALE entry.
  2. Bound to one trace.The sidecar records its source format, the trace's length and a CRC-32 of the trace's own table of contents: the directory of a VTR file, and the header block plus the trailing geometry and hierarchy blocks of an FST. It also records the index-format version. The reader loads the sidecar only when all of these match, so an index left beside a re-simulated trace is ignored and rebuilt, never trusted. When the trace's directory is read-only, the sidecar goes to the user cache (~/.cache/vtr/index/, keyed by the same identity), and the reader looks in both places. A trace that is still being written, or was recovered without its trailer, has no stable identity and is not indexed until it is closed.
  3. One builder, two front ends.vtr::activity holds the format-neutral part: the sidecar format, the loader and query, and a Builder fed block by block (begin_block(start, end, bytes), then change(signal, time) in time order, then end_block()). It never sees values. The VTR front end lives in vtr beside the reader. The FST front end lives in the vtr-cli library beside its FST converter, uses fst-reader, which Volna and the CLI already depend on, and treats each value-change block as a block. The threshold rule is the same because it reads only block bytes, record counts and time spans. Whichever tool wrote a file, its blocks are short where it is busy, since FST writers also flush on buffered data. Semantics match across formats: a change is a recorded value after the first time step, as waveform rows draw it, and an FST block's initial-value frame is not a change.
  4. Built in bounded memory.A trace can decompress to terabytes, so the builder never holds the trace, a block's values or the index in memory. It keeps 24 bytes per signal: the last change time, the current stretch's start and its largest gap. Per block it keeps a 64-bucket silence histogram, and per worker thread one block's time table and the data it is decoding. The VTR front end decodes one 64 KiB column run at a time and parses only change times, so a 2,048-bit bus costs the same as a single bit. The FST front end decodes one value-change block at a time for a bounded set of handles at once. Its block size was chosen by the FST writer, so the builder reads every block's length from its section header first, and refuses a block larger than its memory limit with an error that names it rather than risking the machine. A gzip-wrapped FST is unwrapped by streaming to a temporary file, never into memory as the viewer does today. Peak memory is thus 24 bytes per signal plus a fixed amount per thread, whatever the length of the run: ten million signals cost 240 MB of state, and a run a thousand times longer costs nothing more.
  5. Written chunk by chunk.A stretch is complete when the silence after it is kept, and that is decided when the block holding the silence's end has its Δ. The builder therefore emits one index section per block as soon as that block is done: the block's Δ, the silences that end in it, and the largest gap of each stretch they close. It appends the section to the sidecar and forgets it. The directory and trailer follow the last section, as in a VTR file. The builder writes to a temporary name and renames it into place only when complete, so an interrupted build leaves nothing a reader would trust, and vtr index simply starts again. Blocks decode in parallel ahead of the sequential stitch, which bounds the prefetch to a few blocks' time tables.
  6. Estimated speed.From measured single-block decode times, a VTR build should take roughly 1 s for C910 and 2–5 s for the 4 GiB traces on 16 threads, and skipping values should make it faster than a full decode. FST builds have not been estimated: they depend on the writer's block size and on fst-reader. The benchmark suite already carries FST twins of every signal workload (*_fstapi_zlib.fst, *_fstcpp_none.fst). The builder's peak memory and speed on both formats, on the largest trace, become checked benchmarks.
  7. Three ways to get one.vtr index run.vtr or vtr index run.fst builds or refreshes the sidecar, for a Makefile step after simulation or a batch of old traces; --check only reports whether a valid one exists. Volna opens a trace without a valid index at once, with counts but no meters, and offers "Build activity index", with the expected time, above the hierarchy. The build runs in the background through the same library function, never by shelling out, and the meters appear when it finishes. Future opens are instant. A setting, hierarchy.activityIndex, is "ask" by default; "always" builds without asking and "never" hides the offer. A remote session asks the server, which builds and caches the sidecar next to its trace and sends it once.
  8. The query sits behind the session."Which signals change in this window" is trace data, so its query belongs in vtr::activity, next to the readers, and not in a viewer. Volna's Session trait gains activity(), which returns the loaded index or None, and resolve_activity(signals, t0, t1). The VTR and FST sessions implement both, the same way they already implement signal loads, so the hierarchy browser never asks which format it has. The loaded index is an immutable shared value, and the C API projects the VTR side one to one.
  9. Two calls, one answer.classify fills an active set and an undecided set from the index. resolve reads only the undecided signals in the one or two blocks the window touches. For VTR, that means their column runs, each decompressed once. For FST, it is one read_signals call filtered to those handles and to the window, which fst-reader answers from the overlapping blocks. A caller that wants a single exact answer calls both. The hierarchy browser shows the first answer at once and the second when it lands.
  10. Local and remote alike.The index is raw trace data, not presentation. The remote transport sends the compressed sidecar once, about 1% of the trace, so panning never waits on the network. Meters and scope counts stay client-side in volna-core.
  11. One pass per frame.When the wave viewport changes, the hierarchy model classifies once per frame, coalesced, off the UI thread and parallel over signals, then counts all scopes with the per-signal +1/−1 lists of the scope-sizes design, built once when meters turn on. Only the visible rows are painted.
  12. Honest while pending.In a window narrower than its blocks' Δ, the meter draws the decided share solid and the undecided share hatched, as in the demo. A quiet scope is drawn faint only when its upper bound is zero. When resolve returns, the meter snaps to the exact value. A result for a window that has since moved is dropped.
  13. Semantics.A change is a recorded value change after the trace's first time step. Counting the initial values would make every signal active in any window that touches the first time step. A scope counts the distinct signals below it, including subscopes. Signals with no change at all are part of the total and never light the meter.
  14. Not undoable.Meters are derived view state. Following the undo rules, they stay out of the journal. The one setting, hierarchy.activityIndex, lives in settings.json.
  15. Tested headlessly.core/vtr/tests checks classify plus resolve against a brute-force scan over random windows, including windows that end exactly on changes and block boundaries and traces whose blocks have very different thresholds. Another test rebuilds the sidecar byte for byte, and rejects one whose trace was replaced, truncated or recovered. A cross-format test builds indexes from a benchmark FST and from its VTR conversion, which carry the same changes. Their thresholds differ with their blocks, but both must agree with the brute-force answer for every window. A memory test builds from a synthetic trace with a cap on the builder's memory and fails if it is exceeded. A volna-core test checks the scope counts, the pending range and the build offer. This page's test checks the demo against exact counts computed from the trace.

Plan

Seven commits, each useful on its own.

The design lands in seven commits, from the bottom layer up. Every commit builds, passes cargo test for the crates it touches, cargo test -p volna-core and volna/volna/check.sh, and updates the tests and documentation it touches. No commit adds a feature flag or code that only a later commit calls. Each stage below says what the user gets and then what the commit contains. Where a stage adds something to the screen, its picture shows only the features of that stage and the ones before, and red rings number the new elements as its list does. The pictures are built by this page from the same C910 index as the demo.

Why this order. Each layer is useful before the next one exists.

  • Scope sizes are a design of their own. The size column that meters divide by, and the per-signal lists that count active signals per scope, come from the scope-sizes design. On gate-level hierarchies of millions of scopes they are a problem in their own right. The meters need its first stage.
  • The index is a command-line tool before it is a viewer feature. vtr index and vtr active give a Makefile step and a scripting answer, such as which units toggle in a power window. They also settle the format, the builder's memory bound and the query's exactness against brute force, with no viewer in the way.
  • FST comes third. It is a second front end to a builder and query that are already tested, so it adds one module and a cross-format test.
  • Meters come fourth. They read sidecars that the command line already makes, so the viewer work is only the session calls, the model and the painting.
  • Building from Volna, remote traces and paging come last. Each removes a step or a limit, and the last only if measurements ask for it.

Stage 1

The index, for VTR

The sidecar format, the bounded-memory builder and the VTR front end, with a command to run them. A simulation flow can index every trace as it closes, and a batch of old traces can be indexed overnight.

Committed

Format
A new SPEC entry for the sidecar: the VTR container with its own magic and version 1, the identity record (source format, trace length, table-of-contents CRC-32, index version) and one section per source block holding its time range, bytes and Δ, the silences that end in it and the largest gap of each stretch they close. A RATIONALE entry records the stretch index, the per-block threshold and the options this page rejects, with their measured numbers. The trace format and the writer do not change.
Core
vtr::activity::{Builder, Index, Identity}: the builder fed with begin_block, change and end_block, the section writer that appends and forgets, the temporary name and the final rename, and the loader. The VTR front end walks the blocks in parallel and reads only change times, one column run at a time.
CLI
vtr index <trace> with --check, --threads and --memory. It writes the sidecar next to the trace, or to the user cache when the directory is read-only, and prints one line of what it made.
Bench
vtr-bench activity drops its own collector and calls the builder. It then runs on the 4 GiB traces in bounded memory, and the suite reports build time, peak memory and sidecar size for every trace.
Tests
core/vtr/tests/activity.rs: a round trip through the loader; a byte-for-byte rebuild; rejection of a sidecar whose trace was replaced, truncated or recovered; the threshold rule on synthetic blocks with very different budgets; a build under a memory cap that fails if the builder exceeds it. The page's claims of this section become checked numbers.

Sizes and thresholds as measured for C910. The build time is not shown because it has not been measured yet.

Stage 2

Queries

The two calls of the design, behind a command that answers exactly. Which units wake up in this window? Is the FPU quiet during the boot loop? The answer lists scopes with their changing and total signals, or the changing signals themselves.

Committed

Core
Index::classify(t0, t1) fills the active and undecided sets; vtr::activity::resolve(reader, signals, t0, t1) reads the undecided signals from the one or two blocks the window touches. The C API projects both one to one in vtr.h.
CLI
vtr active <trace> <t0> <t1> with --scope, --depth and --signals. It uses a valid sidecar, or says how to make one and exits with an error, unless --build asks it to build first.
Tests
classify plus resolve against a brute-force scan over random windows, including windows that end exactly on changes and on block boundaries and traces with very different thresholds per block. A CLI test in tools/vtr-cli/tests.

Stage 3

FST traces

vtr index run.fst writes run.fst.index, and vtr active answers on FST files. The answer is the trace's, not the format's: an FST and a VTR of one run give the same lines.

Committed

Core
vtr_cli::fst::activity: the FST front end over fst-reader. It reads every value-change block's length first and refuses a block over the memory limit, unwraps a gzip-wrapped file by streaming to a temporary file, treats each block's initial-value frame as no change, and feeds the same builder. Its identity is the header block plus the trailing geometry and hierarchy blocks. FST resolve is one read_signals call, filtered to the undecided handles and the window.
CLI
Both commands take either format, and detect it as vtr info does.
Bench
Build time and peak memory on the FST twins of every signal workload, which fills in the FST numbers this page does not have yet.
Tests
The cross-format test: an index from a benchmark FST and one from its VTR conversion, each checked against the brute-force answer in every window. A refused oversized block and a gzip-wrapped file.

Stage 4

Meters

A trace opened with a valid sidecar shows the share of each scope's signals that change in the wave viewport, as the demo above does. The meters follow every pan and zoom. A trace without a sidecar keeps its size column from the scope-sizes design.

  1. Changing of total: the count column now reads the signals that change in the viewport, over the scope's size.
  2. A meter on every row, drawn solid for the decided share.
  3. A range while pending. In a window narrower than its blocks' Δ, the undecided share is hatched and the count is a range. It snaps to the exact value when the read returns.
  4. Quiet scopes are faint, but only when no signal below them can change.

Committed

Core
The Session trait gains activity(), returning the loaded index or None, and resolve_activity(signals, t0, t1). The VTR and FST sessions load the sidecar at open when its identity matches; remote sessions return None until stage 6. sidebar::activity::ActivityModel classifies once per frame, coalesced, off the UI thread, and then counts active signals per scope with the per-signal lists of vtr::census from the scope-sizes design, measured at 72 ms per frame for 53 million variables. The resolve runs as a second load request, and a result for a window that has since moved is dropped.
Frontends

Unchanged.

Stage 6

Remote traces

A remote session asks the server for the sidecar. The server builds it next to its trace if needed, caches it for every client, and sends it once, compressed. Panning never waits on the network, because classifying runs on the client.

Limits

What it costs, stated plainly.

Narrow windows are not free. In a window narrower than its blocks' Δ, some signals need the trace, from a few percent on a regular design to nearly all of them on sparse or very wide traces, and the exact meter arrives after one read of at most two blocks. The floor above shows this cannot be removed: exactness at one time unit costs the trace itself. A resolve that caches the decompressed runs of the current blocks would make later pans inside them cost only a column scan. This has not been measured yet.

The first open of an unindexed trace waits for the build. Until the sidecar exists, rows show counts without meters. The build is one streaming pass, so it grows with the trace: estimated at seconds on 16 threads for the 4 GiB traces here. A flow that runs vtr index after simulation never shows the wait.

Well-compressed traces get small indexes. The budget is a share of the file, so a trace that compresses to 0.07 bytes per change, like scr1_x8, keeps only silences longer than about a sixth of a block. That keeps the memory promise but leaves most narrow windows to the read. A viewer with memory to spare could raise the share for small files; that would be a reader option, not a format change.

The loaded index grows with the file. Building is bounded, but a viewer that loads the whole sidecar holds up to 4% of the compressed trace, packed, where the cap does not bind. A 4 GiB trace of busy signals may need up to 160 MiB; the ones measured here need almost nothing, because their signals change on every sample. For traces where even that is too much, the per-block sections allow paging. Each section would repeat the stretches that cross its block's start, so the reader loads only the sections the window touches, plus one bit per signal per block for the interior. That makes the reader's memory bounded like the builder's. It costs the repeated stretches, which have not been measured.

Yes or no, not how much. The meter shows how many signals change, not how often. Activity strength would need change counts per stretch, a different question with a different floor, and this design does not answer it.

Research

Nobody answers this exactly today.

None of the open viewers we read, and no commercial manual we could find, has a per-scope activity display or an exact query for which signals change in a range. The closest pieces of prior art are listed here.

FST stores one position per variable per value-change block, zero when the variable has no changes there. That is the per-block bit of the options table, at block resolution. PulseView keeps ten mipmap levels of ×16, each entry the OR of its children's change bits, and uses them to skip ahead while drawing. Perfetto summarises counters as min, max and last per bucket. Trace Compass's state history tree stores state intervals on disk for time-range queries, the storage closest to a list of silences. Surfer and wellen load only the signals on the canvas.

SAIF gives power tools per-net toggle counts and time at 0, 1 and X, per instance, but for one fixed window per file. Emulation power tools profile activity over billions of cycles to find the busy windows, then analyse only those. The range filters Memento, Grafite and Rosetta answer range emptiness with at least log2(R/ε) bits per key. That fits when keys are few and ranges short. Here the keys are the changes, hundreds of millions to billions of them. The stretch index turns the problem round: it keeps the complement, the few long silences, and its negatives are exact. Elias–Fano coding is the natural packing if the stretch lists ever need to shrink further. Distinct counting over the tree is Hui's colour set size problem (CPM 1992).
